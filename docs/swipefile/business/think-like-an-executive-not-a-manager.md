# Think Like an Executive, Not a Manager

The best model I have found so far is that you have to be able to think like an executive. Not a manager, but an actual executive of a large organization.

Why is that?

Well, executives work at a scale where they can’t see all of the work being done, often don’t understand it as well as the people working for them, can’t communicate with everyone all the time (listening or directing) and often have to manage complex and contradictory incentives.

And mostly, executives have to know what they want and have purpose and coherence in asking for it.

## Working with Larger AI Systems

All of these apply to working with larger AI systems.

You are the scarce resource, so you can’t really pay attention to everything (at least if you want to scale at all).

You have to learn to ask questions, set metrics, be skeptical, learn what you need to learn and how to learn it, know what you don’t know and when it’s important to educate yourself, work via lieutenants, process and metrics, and communicate effectively across a broad set of actors.

## Think Like an Executive: 10 Principles

1. **Operate at the level of outcomes, not activities.** An executive is accountable for the direction and results of the organization, not for personally tracking or understanding every task being performed. Shift attention from monitoring work to ensuring that the right work is happening.

2. **Recognize that attention is the scarce resource.** As organizational scale increases, the volume of decisions, information, dependencies, and actors grows beyond any one person's capacity. Trying to stay involved in everything is not diligence; it is a failure to scale.

3. **Create clarity where visibility is limited.** You cannot see all the work, and you may not understand every technical detail as well as the people closest to it. Compensate through clear intent, strong questions, reliable signals, and structured mechanisms for surfacing problems.

4. **Lead through questions, not constant direction.** Ask questions that expose assumptions, clarify trade-offs, test reasoning, and reveal gaps. The quality of your questions often determines the quality of decisions made throughout the organization.

5. **Build an operating system around metrics and process.** Define what success looks like, establish meaningful measures, create review mechanisms, and use consistent processes. Metrics should provide visibility into outcomes and risks—not become a substitute for judgment.

6. **Delegate through trusted lieutenants.** Build capable leaders who can own substantial areas of responsibility, make decisions, and communicate what matters. Delegation is not merely distributing tasks; it is multiplying organizational judgment and execution capacity.

7. **Know what you don't know—and what you need to learn.** Be intellectually honest about your knowledge gaps. Distinguish between details you can safely delegate and areas where insufficient understanding could compromise an important decision. Learn selectively and deliberately.

8. **Maintain healthy skepticism.** Do not confuse confidence, activity, or polished reporting with actual progress. Challenge assumptions, examine evidence, look for contradictory signals, and create an environment where inconvenient truths can surface.

9. **Create coherence across competing incentives and actors.** Large organizations contain teams with different priorities, perspectives, and incentives. Your job is to establish shared purpose, make trade-offs explicit, and communicate consistently so that independent groups can move in the same direction.

10. **Scale yourself through leverage, not effort.** The ultimate test of executive effectiveness is whether the organization can make good decisions and deliver results without your direct involvement in every detail. Your leverage comes from people, clarity, systems, metrics, and communication.

**The central idea:** A manager scales by managing more work. An executive scales by building an organization that can think, decide, and execute effectively without requiring their attention everywhere.

This is also a useful lens for leading large AI systems: the human should increasingly focus on intent, orchestration, evaluation, exceptions, and accountability—not micromanaging every operation.
